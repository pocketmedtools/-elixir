# Points a doctor should check before the exam

Written by the authors as they wrote, and kept here rather than buried in the
content, because these are the lines where guidance genuinely diverges or where
the author was not certain. Each is already worded in the app as contested
rather than settled — this file is the list to take to a current primary source,
not a list of known errors.

## surgery-office-procedural-sedation-ketamine

- **Raised intracranial pressure.** The classical absolute contraindication
  (still in Indian texts, from 1970s spontaneously-breathing studies in
  obstructive hydrocephalus) against current systematic reviews in sedated,
  ventilated, normocapnic patients showing no rise in ICP and often a better
  cerebral perfusion pressure. The text keeps a residual caution for undrained
  obstructive hydrocephalus in a spontaneously breathing patient.
- **Open globe and intraocular pressure.** Same shape of disagreement: a modest
  transient rise, arguably not clinically significant, but the text still advises
  against ketamine in penetrating eye injury when an alternative exists.
  Examiner expectations vary here more than the evidence does.
- **Porphyria.** The least certain line in the topic: written as "traditionally
  listed as porphyrinogenic and avoided, though the evidence is weak".
  Classification lists disagree — some rate ketamine probably safe, others
  possibly porphyrinogenic.
- **Fasting.** ASA elective thresholds against the ACEP position that recent
  food intake is not a contraindication to emergency procedural sedation,
  presented as a real divergence. The newer one-hour clear-fluid paediatric
  practice is noted but not asserted as the standard.
- **Intranasal ketamine 3–6 mg/kg.** The published range is wide and the effect
  unreliable; labelled as such rather than offered as equivalent to IV or IM.
- **Age cut-off.** ACEP's absolute under-3-months is used. Some sources apply a
  relative caution under 12 months; the higher airway-complication rate in
  infants is stated without making 12 months a second cut-off.
- **Schedule X / NDPS status in India.** Included as a practical availability
  point. The regulatory detail shifts with amendments and is worth re-checking
  each cycle.

## neurology-parkinsonism

- **Early falls.** Classic teaching is that falls in the first year suggest
  progressive supranuclear palsy; the MDS 2015 red flag is recurrent
  balance-related falls within three years. Both are given rather than one.
- **What counts as an adequate levodopa trial.** MDS says "high-dose levodopa";
  units use anywhere from 600 mg/day to 1000–1500 mg/day for four to eight
  weeks. Written as a range, with the variation stated.
- **Indian prevalence.** Door-to-door surveys range roughly 30–70 per 100,000,
  with the Parsi cohort in Mumbai at 328 per 100,000. Given as a range.
- **Young-onset definition** — under 40 or under 50, juvenile under 21 — stated
  as variously defined.
- **Impulse control disorders "roughly one in six".** The DOMINION figure of
  about 17% is for dopaminergic-treated Parkinson's disease overall, with
  agonists the dominant risk factor; the text attaches it to agonists. A fair
  teaching number, not a clean agonist-specific rate.
- **Domperidone.** Given as 10 mg three times daily with a QT caution, without
  the EMA restriction (maximum 30 mg/day, shortest possible duration), because
  Indian practice routinely continues it longer under supervision. Worth a
  reviewer's eye.
- **Trihexyphenidyl.** Capped at 4–6 mg/day in practice (rarely 6–10); product
  labels permit up to 15 mg/day, which is deliberately not quoted.
- **Drug-induced parkinsonism figures** — 10–20% persisting or unmasking
  Parkinson's disease, resolution taking up to 6–18 months — vary between series
  and are phrased as approximations.

## surgery-office-spinal-anaesthesia

- **Caesarean dose of 0.5% heavy bupivacaine.** Given as 2.0–2.2 mL (10–11 mg)
  with fentanyl 15–25 mcg, with the honest range 1.8–2.5 mL (9–12.5 mg) and the
  ED95 of about 11–13 mg without an opioid stated alongside. Indian textbooks
  commonly teach 2–2.5 mL; obstetric practice elsewhere trends lower with an
  opioid. This is the number a marker is most likely to disagree with, so the
  range is written out rather than a single figure.
- **Absolute against relative contraindications.** Severe aortic stenosis and
  fixed cardiac output states, and demyelinating disease, are listed as absolute
  by some texts and relative by others. They sit under relative here, with the
  disagreement stated in the line — an examiner using Ajay Yadav may expect
  severe aortic stenosis under absolute.
- **ASRA 4th edition (2018) clopidogrel interval.** ASRA states 7 days, allowing
  5 if platelet function testing is normal; the text says 5–7 days, which is what
  most Indian teaching says, and notes that ESAIC intervals differ slightly and
  that the guideline followed should be named.
- **Maximum safe dose of bupivacaine.** 2 mg/kg, or 2.5 mg/kg with adrenaline,
  explicitly for infiltration and epidural rather than the intrathecal dose.
  Some texts quote 3 mg/kg with adrenaline.
- **Permanent neurological injury rate.** Given as of the order of 1 in 20,000
  to 1 in 50,000 or less, and vertebral canal haematoma as about 1:150,000 to
  1:220,000 after spinal. These are NAP3-era figures and denominators vary
  considerably between series, so they are framed as orders of magnitude.

## surgery-office-blood-transfusion

- **Edition year of the NBTC/NACO standards.** Cited as the 2022 edition of the
  *Standards for Blood Banks and Blood Transfusion Services*, alongside the NACO
  *Transfusion Medicine Technical Manual* 2e (2013). The 2007 standards with
  later revisions are the ones most often quoted, and the post-2020 document is
  titled "blood centres" rather than "blood banks". The year is the part to
  check.
- **Anti-D dose convention.** 300 microgram (1500 IU) at 28 weeks and within 72
  hours of delivery — Indian and US practice. UK/BSH regimens differ (a single
  1500 IU at 28–30 weeks, or 500 IU at 28 and 34 weeks) and are not spelt out.
- **NAT status in India.** Written as not nationally mandatory but widely
  adopted and mandated in some states, with the line saying so explicitly
  because this is a moving target.
- **Donor inter-donation interval.** The NBTC standard of 3 months for both
  sexes is used, with the international 12/16-week variant noted in the line.
- **Transfusion threshold in acute myocardial infarction.** The 8 g/dL
  perioperative and cardiac figure is given as the rule, with the MINT trial
  (2023) and the move back towards about 10 g/dL in acute MI noted in the same
  line, because guidance genuinely diverges here.
- **Fresh frozen plasma freezing window** written as "within 6–8 hours" to cover
  the NBTC 6-hour figure and the AABB 8-hour figure rather than committing.
- **Indian ABO frequencies** given as ranges (B and O each roughly a third)
  because published Indian series differ regionally on which predominates.

## surgery-office-tracheostomy

- **The first tube change.** Genuinely unsettled, and written as varying: day
  3–5 where maturation or stay sutures were placed, day 5–7 for most surgical
  stomas, 7–10 days after a percutaneous tracheostomy. The principle — an
  epithelialised tract, and the risk of a false passage — is the fixed part.
- **Suction negative pressure.** Sources split between 80–120 mmHg and
  100–150 mmHg (13–20 kPa). The Intensive Care Society figure is used
  (100–150 mmHg in adults, 80–100 mmHg in children).
- **Suction catheter size.** The formula (Fr no more than 2 × internal diameter
  in mm − 2) and the "should occlude less than half the lumen" rule do not quite
  agree at an 8.0 mm tube, so only the formula is quoted, with a worked example.
- **Conversion of a cricothyroidotomy at 3–7 days** and the **under-12 cut-off**
  for the surgical rather than needle technique are traditional teaching with
  weak evidence and a range across texts (8–12 years); the weak evidence is
  flagged in the text and the cut-off written as "about 12 years".
- **Cuff pressure** given as 20–30 cmH2O, justified against tracheal mucosal
  capillary perfusion pressure; some units teach 20–25.
- **Jackson's low tracheostomy.** Dhingra treats it as rarely performed rather
  than giving crisp indications, so it is framed as reserved for special
  situations rather than given an invented list.

## neurology-seizures-epilepsy

- **Driving in India.** No single defensible national seizure-free interval could
  be pinned down. The Central Motor Vehicles Rules Form 1 self-declaration has
  historically been an absolute bar, with amendments and Indian Epilepsy Society
  advocacy moving towards a defined interval, applied inconsistently. The text
  says so explicitly and anchors the practical advice to the UK DVLA numbers
  (one year for established epilepsy, six months after a single unprovoked
  seizure with normal investigations, no driving during and for six months after
  drug withdrawal, never a commercial vehicle) — labelled as UK figures, not
  presented as Indian law.
- **Ethosuximide availability in India** is genuinely patchy, and many Indian
  units start valproate for that reason. Stated as a practical caveat rather
  than pretending the drug is always obtainable.
- **NICE NG217 ordering in absence seizures.** Ethosuximide first, valproate
  second (subject to the MHRA valproate restrictions), lamotrigine or
  levetiracetam if both fail — but valproate moves first where generalised
  tonic-clonic seizures coexist, except in girls. Written as that conditional
  rather than a flat ladder.
- **Vitamin K in late pregnancy on enzyme-inducing drugs.** Evidence is weak and
  guidelines have diverged; omitted rather than given a number.
- **HLA-B*1502 and carbamazepine.** Firm in Han Chinese and Thai populations;
  allele frequency in Indian populations is reported but lower and testing is
  not routine here. Phrased as a caution in some Asian populations rather than a
  testing recommendation.
- **Ethosuximide paediatric maintenance.** Sources differ (BNFC about
  20 mg/kg/day, Nelson 20–30, some to 40). 20–30 mg/kg/day to a maximum of
  1500 mg/day is used.
